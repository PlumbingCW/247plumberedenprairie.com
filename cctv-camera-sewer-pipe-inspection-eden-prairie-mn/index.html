Sewer Camera Inspection for Eden Prairie, MN Homes

A sewer camera inspection lets a plumber watch the inside of your building sewer on a monitor, so repair decisions rest on footage instead of guesses about what the pipe looks like.

By the Eden Prairie 24/7 Plumbing team · Updated October 16, 2026

A sewer camera inspection in Eden Prairie, MN means feeding a waterproof camera head through a cleanout and down your building sewer while a plumber watches a live monitor. It shows roots, cracks, sags and offset joints without digging. Book one when buying a house, after repeat backups, or before anyone talks about lining the pipe. It cannot see through a full blockage, and it does not test for leaks.

How the camera gets into the pipe

The camera head is about the size of a thumb or a small can, ringed with bright LEDs and sealed against water. It rides on a flexible push cable wound on a reel, or on a small wheeled crawler for larger lines. A cable counter or a transmitter in the head tells the operator how far in the camera sits.

Most visits start at a cleanout, the capped fitting on the building sewer, often in a basement floor, a crawl space or just outside the foundation. If there is no cleanout, a plumber may pull a toilet to get in. That adds time, so mention it when you call.

Push camera or crawler: which one fits

A push camera suits the usual 3 or 4 inch residential line. The cable is stiff enough to shove the head around bends, and the unit is light enough to carry down basement stairs. For a long run to the street, a heavier crawler with its own wheels can keep the lens level and travel farther.

Neither type is better in general. The right one depends on pipe diameter, the number of turns, and how much debris sits in the line. A plumber will usually clear loose buildup first so the lens has something to look at.

What the footage can show

A clear run of clay, cast iron, PVC or ABS looks smooth, with joints appearing as a faint ring at regular spacing. Trouble stands out against that pattern.

  • Fine root hairs entering at joints, or thick root masses filling the pipe
  • Cracks, breaks or a collapsed section where the wall caves inward
  • Offset joints where one pipe length has slipped off the next
  • Scale, grease or debris narrowing the bore
  • A belly, where the pipe sags and holds standing water

Reading the footage like a homeowner

Ask for the recording and the depth reading, not just a verbal summary. A good report names the distance from the cleanout, the pipe material, and the type of defect at each spot. A sag at 20 feet and a root intrusion at 55 feet call for different fixes, and the distances let a repair crew find them again.

Pay attention to how bad the defect looks relative to how the line behaves. Light root hairs in a pipe that has never backed up can often wait and be monitored. A cracked section that already leaks soil into the line is a different conversation.

Three good times to book one

The first is before you close on a house. A camera look costs a small part of a sewer replacement, and it tells you whether the seller's line is sound. Older homes and homes with mature trees near the yard deserve the most scrutiny.

The second is after a repeat backup. If a drain clears and then clogs again in a few weeks, something in the pipe is catching debris. The third is before lining or any trenchless plan, because the installer needs to know the pipe's route, diameter and condition first. Our sewer inspection visits are built around those three cases.

What a camera cannot see

A camera sees only the inside surface of the pipe it can reach. It cannot see through water that fills the pipe, through a solid plug of roots, or past a collapse that blocks the head. In those cases a plumber clears the line first and then re-runs the camera.

It also does not prove a pipe is watertight. A hairline crack that only weeps during heavy rain may look like nothing on screen. And it says nothing about the water supply side: for pressurized lines, you want leak detection, not a sewer camera.

Preparing for the visit

A little prep saves time on site. Clear a path to the cleanout.

  1. Find your cleanout cap and note where it is.
  2. Stop running water and the dishwasher an hour before the visit, so the pipe is not flowing.
  3. Write down which drains have clogged, and when.
  4. Ask whether the recording will be sent to you afterward.

The City does not state who owns a home's sewer service line, so ask the Utilities Division before assuming the repair is yours or theirs.

When the footage points to repair

If the recording shows a break, a bad sag or heavy root damage, the options run from spot repair to full replacement. A short failure near the house may be a small dig. A long run of damaged clay may be better served by lining or pipe bursting, if the installer's camera work says the route allows it.
