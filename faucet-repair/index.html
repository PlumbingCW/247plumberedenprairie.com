Why faucets fail in Eden Prairie homes

Most Eden Prairie homes date from the 1970s onward, so many original faucets, cartridges and chrome stop valves are well past a decade of use. The rubber seals inside harden, and valve stems pit. Fixtures from that era also tend to use compression stems, which wear faster than modern cartridges.

Water chemistry plays a smaller part. The City softens its well water, which averages about 5 grains per gallon at the tap, so heavy scale is less of a problem than in harder towns. Calcium still builds on aerators and spouts over time, and the City suggests cleaning calcium buildup with vinegar or a calcium and lime remover.

What you see and what it means

Faucet problems we are called for

A steady drip from the spout

The cartridge, washer or valve seat inside has worn. Tightening the handle will not fix it.

Low flow from one tap only

Debris is probably clogging the aerator or the cartridge. If both hot and cold are weak, check the stop valves.

Water at the base of the handle

An O-ring or packing nut has failed, and the leak runs into the countertop.

A shower that will not shut off

The shower cartridge or valve body is worn, or the diverter is stuck.

A stop valve that will not turn

Mineral buildup or old packing can freeze the stem, and forcing it can snap the valve.

Cartridges, aerators and O-rings

The cartridge is the heart of a single-handle faucet. It controls both flow and temperature, and when it wears the handle drips, feels gritty or runs lukewarm. A plumber matches the replacement to the brand and model, pulls the old one with the correct clip or nut, and reseals the O-rings with plumber's grease.

The aerator is the small screen at the tip of the spout. Unscrew it, soak it in vinegar, and brush out the grit. If water pressure feels weak at a single tap, clearing that screen is the cheapest test before any parts are ordered.

When a supply valve or line is an emergency

A stop valve that will not close, or a braided supply line splitting under the sink, can flood a cabinet in minutes. Close the main at your meter if the stop valve fails. The City says most meters have two valves, one on each side, and sit inside the home, usually near the water heater in the basement.

We handle after-hours and weekend calls for burst supply lines, and same-day appointments are offered when available for stuck shutoffs that have not failed yet. Replace stop valves while the water is off. Old multi-turn valves that have not been exercised in years often fail the first time you close them.

Shower valves and showerheads

A showerhead that drips after the shower is off usually has a worn cartridge or a tired diverter. A leaking showerhead connection can often be fixed with a new washer and thread tape. Water behind the wall is different: a stain on the ceiling below, a soft spot or a high water bill suggests a valve body or pipe leak. Our leak detection page explains how hidden leaks are located.

Bathtub faucets, spouts and diverters

The tub spout carries a diverter, a small gate or pull-up knob that sends water to the shower. When it wears, water dribbles from the spout during a shower. Replacing the spout is often quick, and it is worth checking for a loose spout pipe that lets water into the wall.

A shower or tub that drains slowly

Hair and soap sit in the tub drain, just below the strainer, and a slow shower is nearly always hair first. A drain tool or a gentle cable pulls it out. If more than one fixture is slow, or the tub gurgles when a toilet flushes, the clog is deeper. See our drain cleaning page for how cables and camera work reach beyond the fixture.
