## Snake, jet or camera first: how to decide
A cable is the right first move for one fixture with a soft clog near the trap. It is the wrong move when the blockage keeps returning, because a cable punches a hole through debris and leaves the walls coated.

Jetting sends pressurized water through a hose and scours the pipe wall, which suits grease, scale and silt. A camera goes in first when you do not know what you are fighting, when several fixtures are involved, or when a cable has already failed once. Our [[hydro-jetting|hydro jetting]] page explains when water beats steel.

## When a clogged drain is an emergency
Some clogs can wait until morning. These cannot:

- Sewage or gray water coming up through a floor drain or basement shower
- Every fixture on the lowest level backing up at once
- Water spreading toward finished flooring, a furnace or a panel

If any of those match, turn off the washer, stop running water, and call. We take after-hours and weekend calls like any other, and same-day appointments are available when the schedule allows.

## Signs the drain needs attention
:::signs What the pipe is telling you
### Slow in one fixture
Usually a hair or soap clog in the trap or the first few feet of branch line.
### Slow in several fixtures
Two or three fixtures dragging together point past the traps to the branch or main line.
### Gurgling after a flush
Air pulled through a trap means the line is partly blocked downstream.
### Water in the shower while the washer runs
Laundry water has nowhere to go, so it rises at the lowest open drain in the house.
:::

## Tubs, showers, laundry drains and floor drains
Bath and shower drains clog with hair wound around the stopper or crossbar, held together by soap film. A drain claw often finishes the job, but deep standing water with a clean stopper means the clog sits lower and needs a machine.

Laundry drains carry lint and detergent residue, and a standpipe that overflows during the spin cycle usually means a lint-packed trap arm. A floor drain that smells is more often a dried-out trap seal than a blockage. Basement floor drains sit at the low point of the house, so they show a deeper problem first.

## Unclogging a drain without making it worse
A plunger with a good seal works on tubs and sinks if you block the overflow opening first. Stop there. Chemical openers sit in standing water, can soften old pipe joints and burn the skin of whoever works on the line next.

## Snaking and cabling: when each tool earns its place
A hand auger handles sink and tub traps. A drum machine with a cutter head clears branch lines and chews through roots and soft scale. Water is the better call for repeat grease clogs. A camera belongs first when you suspect a break, a sag or a foreign object, because the picture tells you whether to cut, flush or dig. For that decision see [[sewer-inspection|sewer inspection]].

## Kitchen sink clog
The kitchen drain takes grease that cools into a wax lining, coffee grounds, rice that swells and fibrous peels. The clog usually forms in the trap arm or at the first elbow in the wall, where flow slows down.

First rule out the disposal. If it hums but does not spin, the jam is in the unit, and our [[garbage-disposal|garbage disposal]] page covers that case. If both basins are full and the disposal runs freely, the blockage is in the drain line.

Remove the trap with a bucket below it, clear it, then run a hand auger into the wall pipe. If a double sink backs up on both sides, a machine with a cutter head clears the wall and the branch beyond.

Grease is the repeat offender. Pour it into a can and bin it, and wipe pans before washing, or the same clog returns every few months.

## When the drain line itself leaks
A drain line that leaks inside a wall or under a slab behaves differently from a clog. You find damp drywall or a musty patch in the basement floor that appears only when water runs. If the spot dries between uses, suspect the drain line rather than a supply pipe. A camera and a flow test locate the break, and [[sewer-line-repair|sewer line repair]] covers the options.

Drain and vent work is covered by the Minnesota Plumbing Code, Minnesota Rules chapter 4714, and a plumber clearing a line works within those standards.

:::faq
Q: Do you handle drain cleaning emergencies at night or on weekends in Eden Prairie?
A: Yes. Dispatch runs 24/7, and overnight and weekend calls are taken the same way as weekday calls. Stop using the drain, turn off the washer, and keep the area clear until a plumber is on site.

Q: Should I snake a drain or jet it?
A: Snake a single fixture with a fresh clog. Jet for grease, scale or repeat clogs, and use a camera first if a cable has already failed.

Q: Is it safe to use a chemical drain cleaner first?
A: We advise against it. Standing caustic water can damage old joints and is hazardous to the next person working on the pipe.

Q: How do I clear a kitchen sink clog that keeps coming back?
A: Remove the trap, then run an auger into the wall. If it returns within weeks, a cutter head or jetting removes the grease lining that a basic cable leaves behind.

Q: Why does my laundry standpipe overflow?
A: The trap arm is usually packed with lint. It needs a cable pass and often a lint filter on the washer hose.

Q: Who is responsible when the line toward the street backs up in Eden Prairie?
A: The City does not state who maintains a home's service line, so ask the Utilities Division. They oversee the sanitary sewer system and can tell you where the maintained section begins.
