## From slow drains to the right method
Hydro jetting is rarely the first thing a homeowner thinks of. It becomes the answer once the symptoms stack up: slow drains in several fixtures at once, a gurgle from a toilet when the washer empties, and a clog that returns every few months after each cable visit.

:::signs Symptoms that point toward jetting
### Slow drains in several fixtures
When the tub, the laundry and a basement sink all drag together, the restriction sits in a shared line, not in one trap.
### Gurgling from a distant fixture
A bubbling toilet or a floor drain that burps means air is fighting past a partial blockage.
### Repeat clogs every few months
The line is coated. A cable opens a channel through the buildup, and the walls stay narrow.
### Roots in the cleanout
Fine hairlike roots on a cable head say the pipe has an opening somewhere.
### Grease smell and slimy residue
A sour, waxy layer on the pipe wall that no cable removes.
:::

## When a backed-up line is an emergency
Call right away when sewage is rising in a floor drain, when a basement shower or tub fills as the washer drains, or when every fixture on the lowest level stops draining at the same moment. These are active backups, and they damage flooring, drywall and stored items quickly. We handle these overnight and on weekends, and same-day appointments are available for slower problems.

## How hydro jetting works
A jetter is a pump and a reel of flexible hose with a nozzle on the end. Water goes through the hose at high pressure, and jets on the nozzle push the hose forward into the pipe and flush debris back toward the opening. The cleaning comes from the water, not from a spinning blade.

Different nozzles do different work:

- A forward-facing nozzle punches through a hard blockage
- A rear-facing nozzle pulls itself along and scours the walls
- A rotating nozzle spins to cut grease, scale and root fibers
- A flat-spray nozzle removes sludge from the bottom of the pipe

The operator works the hose in passes, going in, then pulling back slowly while the jets clean the wall. Pulling back is where most of the scrubbing happens.

## Camera first, then water
A camera run before jetting shows what you are cleaning. Look for a belly (a sag that holds water), an offset joint, a crack or a collapsed length. Each of these changes the plan. Our [[sewer-inspection|sewer inspection]] page walks through what the picture looks like.

Skipping the camera is how a jetter ends up pushing water at a broken pipe, and a jet can wash soil through a failed section.

## When not to jet
Jetting is not right for every line. Avoid it when the pipe is cracked, collapsed or badly bellied, or when it is old, thin cast iron that may be fragile. Clay tile with loose joints and Orangeburg (a bonded fiber pipe) can also come apart under pressure.

When a camera finds those conditions, the honest answer is cabling with a gentle head or repair. See [[sewer-line-repair|sewer line repair]] for the options once the pipe is exposed.

## Jetting versus cabling
:::table
What you want | Jetting | Cabling
Remove grease and scale from the wall | Strong, scours the full circumference | Weak, opens a channel only
Cut a thick root mass | Good with a cutting nozzle | Good with a cutter head
Break a hard object or toy | Limited | Better, can hook or break it
Fragile or failed pipe | Risky, camera first | Gentler if done carefully
:::
A one-time clog is often a cable job, and a line that clogs again and again needs the walls cleaned. Many visits use both. Everyday sink and shower work is covered under [[drain-cleaning|drain cleaning]].

## Why Eden Prairie lines build up
Per the City, most Eden Prairie homes date from the 1970s onward, with a few older farm-era buildings. Cast iron drain branches from that era develop rough scale that catches grease and paper, while newer plastic lines can hold a film of fat and settle into a slight sag. Both are good jetting candidates once a camera confirms the pipe is sound.

Roots find any joint that leaks a little moisture. If they keep returning, jetting clears them and the camera tells you where the pipe is open. Roots in a line toward the street raise a separate question: ask the City's Utilities Division who maintains that stretch, since the City does not state who maintains a home's service line.

## Preparing for a jetting visit
:::steps
Locate the cleanout: a capped pipe near the foundation, in the basement or in the yard.
Clear access: move stored items, shelving and laundry baskets away from the work area.
Stop water use: pause the washer, dishwasher and showers before the plumber starts.
Protect the floor: lay down towels near the opening, since water and debris can surge out.
Note the history: tell the plumber about earlier clogs, roots or a previous camera finding.
:::

## Where the debris goes
Jetting does not make grease disappear. It breaks scale and fat into pieces small enough to flow downstream through the sewer, and loose sediment is pulled back toward the access point where a plumber can remove it. Heavy grease loads in commercial kitchens are a separate maintenance issue, and our [[commercial-plumbing|commercial plumbing]] page covers them.

## Questions about jetting
Jetting is a cleaning method, not a repair, and any piping work that follows it falls under the standards of the Minnesota Plumbing Code, Minnesota Rules chapter 4714.

## What not to do before a jetting visit
Do not use chemical drain cleaners in a clogged line, because they can burn skin and eyes when the plumber opens the pipe. Do not run water into a backed-up drain, and keep people and pets away from wet floors near the cleanout.

A: Yes. Dispatch runs 24/7, including overnight and weekends. Stop using water in the house and tell the dispatcher which fixtures are backing up.

Q: Will hydro jetting damage my pipes?
A: On sound pipe, no. On cracked, collapsed or very old fragile pipe it can do harm, which is why we put a camera in first when we can.

Q: How is jetting different from snaking?
A: A snake opens a hole through the blockage. A jet uses water to clean the whole pipe wall, so it suits grease, scale and repeat clogs.

Q: How often should a line be jetted?
A: It depends on the cause. Grease lines may need regular cleaning, while a line cleared of roots might go much longer if the pipe is sound.

Q: Can jetting clear tree roots?
A: Yes, with the right nozzle, but roots return if the pipe has an opening. A camera shows where, and repair is the lasting fix.
