Garbage disposal installation in Eden Prairie, MN is a job a handy homeowner can do in a few hours when the sink drain is sound and a power outlet is already under the sink. A store install service handles the simple swap, but usually stops at the disposal itself. A plumber covers the trap, the drain line and the wiring when something is off. Pick the route based on what the cabinet looks like, not the sticker price.

## Three ways to get it installed
Most homeowners choose between a store-arranged installer, a DIY swap, or a plumber hired directly. They overlap but differ in scope.

A store install service is often a subcontracted installer who mounts the unit you bought. It tends to be limited to a like-for-like swap, with extra charges for anything outside that scope. A DIY swap saves labor if the old unit comes out cleanly. A plumber installs the disposal and repairs the surrounding drain pieces in one visit, which matters when the old setup is not in good shape.

## What the job involves
A disposal bolts to the sink flange with a mounting ring and snap ring. The discharge port connects to the trap through a short pipe. A dishwasher, if present, ties in through a drain hose at a side port.

The steps run in a predictable order.

1. Turn off power at the breaker and unplug or disconnect the old unit.
2. Disconnect the trap and dishwasher hose, then lift out the old disposal.
3. Clean the sink flange and set the new mounting ring.
4. Hang the new unit, align the discharge and tighten the clamps.
5. Reconnect the trap, dishwasher hose and power, then run water and check for leaks.

## The dishwasher knockout plug
Most new disposals ship with a plastic plug in the dishwasher inlet. If a dishwasher drains through the disposal, that plug must be knocked out before the hose is attached. Forget it and the dishwasher cannot drain, which shows up as standing dirty water in the machine.

Tap the plug with a screwdriver and hammer from the inside, and fish the loose piece out of the chamber. If there is no dishwasher, leave the plug in. A missed or half-removed plug is one of the most common reasons for a post-install callback.

## Electrical: hardwired or cord
Disposals connect in one of two ways. A power cord plugs into an outlet under the sink, often switched on the wall. A hardwired unit connects directly to a junction box. Both need the breaker off before work.

A cord kit and a grounded outlet are simple to set up. Hardwiring takes more care with the ground wire and cable clamps. If you are not sure how the old unit was wired, or the outlet is missing, stop and ask a licensed electrician or plumber to evaluate it. Plumbing work does not cover new circuits.

## Horsepower and sizing
Disposals range from about one third horsepower to one horsepower or more. Higher horsepower grinds faster, runs quieter in many models, and handles tougher scraps, but it costs more and takes more room under the sink.

For a small household that cooks lightly, a mid-range unit is plenty. For a family that grinds a lot of food scraps, a stronger motor with a continuous-feed design avoids jams. Check cabinet clearance and the height of the trap before you buy.

## When the install exposes a bad trap
The old disposal often hides a trap that is corroded, cracked or sloped the wrong way. The moment you remove it, you see black sludge, crusty fittings or a leaking slip joint.

This is where DIY plans change. A new disposal on a bad trap will leak or clog. A plumber replaces the trap arm, fixes the slope, and checks the wall stub for rust. ## Running it correctly after install
Run cold water before, during and for several seconds after grinding. Cold water keeps fat solid so it flushes through as bits instead of coating the pipe. Feed scraps in small amounts, not a heap.

Avoid grease, coffee grounds in large quantities, bones, fruit pits and potato peels in bulk. Never put your hand in the chamber. If the unit hums but does not spin, shut off power and use the hex slot underneath to free it.

## Questions to ask any installer
Ask who handles the old unit's disposal and haul-away, and whether the visit covers a replacement trap or only the mounting. Ask what happens if the sink flange is cracked or the cabinet wiring is not up to the job, since both are common surprises.

Also ask whether the work is done by a licensed plumber. Minnesota's Department of Labor and Industry offers a license and registration lookup for homeowners, so you can verify a plumber before the visit.

:::faq
Q: Can I install a garbage disposal myself?
A: Yes, if the sink drain is sound and the electrical setup is ready. Plan a few hours and have a bucket, plumber's putty or gasket, and a screwdriver ready. If the trap or wiring looks wrong, stop and call.

Q: Does a new disposal come with a power cord?
A: Many do not. Some include a cord kit and others expect hardwiring. Check the box before you buy.

Q: How do I know if a disposal is the right size?
A: Match the motor to your household and sink space. Light use needs less power. Heavy cooking and larger families benefit from a stronger motor.

Q: Why is my new disposal leaking?
A: The usual causes are a loose mounting ring, a missing gasket, a bad slip joint or a trap in poor shape. Tighten the connections once, and if it keeps weeping, call.
