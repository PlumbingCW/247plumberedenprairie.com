Chanhassen is the neighbor where the water itself changes the plumbing advice. The City of Chanhassen says its groundwater contains naturally occurring iron, manganese and hardness, and that treatment removes nearly all the iron and manganese but does not reduce hardness. Its listed hardness is 21 grains per gallon, and the city does not soften the water.

That is a sharp contrast with Eden Prairie, where the City softens tap water to about 5 grains per gallon. If you moved from one to the other, or you split time between them, your fixtures, heater and softener settings are not interchangeable. Our dispatch line sits in Eden Prairie and answers for Chanhassen around the clock.

## Water first: the problems 21 grains per gallon creates
Hard water leaves calcium on faucet aerators, showerheads and the heating elements of electric water heaters. The city's own page notes the spots on dishes and the buildup on fixtures, and says most residents benefit from a home water softener. The practical result is that Chanhassen heaters often fail from sediment and scale before they fail from rust.

Here is what we check on a call about no hot water or a rumbling tank. We flush the tank, test the elements or burner, inspect the anode rod, and look at the temperature and pressure relief valve for crusting. A scaled-up tank may be worth a [[water-heater-repair|repair]], while a tank with a leaking seam needs replacement.

## Where Chanhassen sits and how we reach you
Chanhassen is southwest of Eden Prairie and mostly in Carver County, with a small part in Hennepin County. U.S. Highway 212 and Minnesota Highways 5 and 41 are the main routes through town. Highway 5 and Highway 212 also run through Eden Prairie, which is why dispatch can reach both ends of the corridor quickly.

The city's population grew from about 4,900 in 1970 to about 11,700 in 1990 and about 20,300 in 2000, then to about 25,900 by 2020, per Census counts. Newer homes mean modern supply lines, but they also mean sump pumps, finished basements and water softeners that need attention.

## Emergencies in Chanhassen: what we see most
In winter the headline calls are burst pipes in exterior walls and garages, along with no-heat events that freeze a loop. In spring it is sump pumps and floor drains. In summer it is irrigation leaks and the water heater that finally quits.

For a failed pump, our [[sump-pump-repair|sump pump repair]] visit covers the float, check valve, discharge and backup. For drains that back up, [[drain-cleaning|drain cleaning]] comes first. And when the whole house has slow drains, a camera inspection tells you whether it is a branch or the main line.

## Before we arrive: if you have a burst pipe or active leak in Chanhassen
:::prep What to do in the next ten minutes
### Close the main valve
Turn the main shutoff fully closed. It is usually on the basement wall where the water line enters the house.
### Cut power to the wet area
Switch off the circuit for a wet utility room or basement only if you can do it from dry ground. If water reaches the panel, stay out and say so on the phone.
### Move the contents
Lift boxes, rugs and electronics out of the way, and open a low faucet to drain the lines.
### Call the 24/7 line
Tell the dispatcher what burst, where the valve is, and whether water is touching anything electrical.
:::

## Insurance and next steps
Most homeowner policies treat sudden bursts differently from gradual leaks. Photograph the damage, keep the failed part and write down when you shut off the water. Your insurer decides coverage, so ask them early.

If you want to know what a repair or a softener would involve for your house, [[call|call (952) 866-9120]] and the dispatcher will explain what the plumber will check and how you will see the price before work starts.

Plumbing work in Chanhassen is held to the same state standard as anywhere else in Minnesota, the Minnesota Plumbing Code, Minnesota Rules chapter 4714.

Q: What sets a Chanhassen water heater apart from one in Eden Prairie?
A: Hard water speeds up scale, so tanks need flushing and anode checks more often. A softener can protect the heater, though salt-softened water is not advised for people on sodium-restricted diets.

Q: Do I need a softener or just a filter?
A: A softener removes hardness minerals, while a filter handles taste, chlorine or sediment. If scale is your problem, a softener helps. If it is taste, a filter is the right tool.

Q: My sump pump runs all the time after rain. Is that normal?
A: Running during a downpour is normal, but constant running on dry days points to a stuck float, a failing check valve or a high water table. Have it checked before the next storm.
