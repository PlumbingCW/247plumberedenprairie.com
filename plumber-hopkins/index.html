Hopkins is small, with about 19,000 residents, and old by suburban standards. A train depot was built here in 1871, a streetcar arrived in 1899, and the village took the Hopkins name in 1928. Minneapolis-Moline made farm equipment in town until the 1960s, long before Eden Prairie filled in.

That age is the reason our Hopkins calls skew toward pipe. Our 24/7 line from Eden Prairie covers the city with plumbers who expect to find mixed materials, patched connections and valves that have not been turned in decades.

Season first: what a Minnesota winter does to Hopkins plumbing

The calls that fill our Hopkins board in January are frozen and split pipes in exterior walls, garages and unheated crawlspaces. The older the house, the more likely a supply line runs through a cold cavity that was never insulated. When a pipe freezes but has not yet split, thawing it carefully saves the wall.

In spring, thaw brings leaks that were hiding in cold weather and basement water from the melt. A burst pipe repair visit covers the shutoff, the damaged section and a plan to prevent a repeat, such as rerouting or insulating the run.

Aging mains and old valves

The city's own water plan says about a quarter of its mains are aging or undersized and slated for replacement over the next two decades.

What Hopkins' water plan tells a plumber

Hopkins gets its water from three active city wells, 500 to 548 feet deep, feeding the Elmo Park Water Treatment Plant, which was built in 1967 and uses gravity filtration and chemicals to remove iron and manganese. The city does not buy water on a regular basis, and its plan lists emergency connections with Minnetonka, St. Louis Park and Edina.

The plan does not report hardness, so we do not quote a figure. For you the safe move is to test your tap and ask the city for its latest water quality report. If you see orange stains at fixtures, mention it on the phone. Staining like that can come from iron in the water or from old galvanized pipe inside your house, and a plumber can tell which.

When a repipe beats another repair

A Hopkins house with galvanized supply lines, repeated pinhole leaks and weak pressure is a repipe candidate. The honest test is the count and the cost: after the second or third leak in a year, new PEX or copper usually costs less than continued repair. Our repiping page explains materials, wall access and how the work is staged.

Not every old house needs a full repipe. A partial replacement of the kitchen and bath branches often fixes the worst problems at a fraction of the disruption, and a plumber should be willing to show you both options with a clear price before work starts.

Before we arrive: if you have a burst pipe or active leak in Hopkins

Four steps that limit the damage

1

Turn off the water

Close the main valve, or the valve on either side of the meter. If it is stuck, say so on the phone and keep your hands off the handle so it does not break.

2

Cut power where it is wet

Shut off breakers to wet rooms only from dry ground. Stay away from outlets and the panel if water is near them.

3

Make space

Lift valuables and open a faucet at the lowest point to drain the lines. Put a bucket under any active drip.

4
