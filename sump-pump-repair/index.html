When a failed sump pump is an emergency

The pit is nearly full and the pump does not start, the pump hums but moves no water, or the pump has been running for hours without lowering the level. Heavy rain, a fast spring thaw and a power outage are the usual triggers, and they often arrive at night or on a weekend. We take after-hours calls for exactly this, because a basement can take on water faster than anyone can bail.

If the pit is full and the power is out, a battery backup, if you have one, may keep going for a while. Buckets and a wet vacuum on a dry circuit can buy time, and a same-day visit when available can follow once the storm passes. If water is already on the floor, do not wait.

What goes wrong with sump pumps

Most failures come from five places: a float switch that sticks or tangles, a motor that burned out after years of running, a check valve that fails and lets water drain back, a clogged intake or discharge line, and a lost power supply. A pump that runs constantly may have a stuck float, a failed check valve or a pit that is refilling because the water table is high.

A noisy or rattling pump often has a worn impeller or debris in the housing. One that cycles every few seconds may have a check valve letting discharge water fall back.

Signs the pump needs attention

It does not turn on

Water rises in the pit and the motor stays silent, which points to a float, a switch or power.

It runs constantly

A stuck float, a failed check valve or a pit that refills as fast as it empties.

Loud grinding or rattling

A worn bearing, debris in the impeller or a pump that has tipped over.

High-water alarms mean the pit is above normal. Check the pump first.

Sewage ejector pumps for basement bathrooms

Many basements have a bathroom below the sewer line, so the toilet, shower and sink drain into a sealed basin and an ejector pump lifts the waste up. It is not the same as a sump pump. A failed ejector can back sewage into the basement or leave the bathroom unusable.

Signs include gurgling, a slow flush, odors from the basin and an alarm. Do not run water in that bathroom if the basin is full. Repair usually means a new pump, float or check valve. A clog in the line above points to drain cleaning.

Check valves, floats and discharge lines

The check valve sits on the discharge pipe and stops water falling back into the pit when the pump shuts off. A failing one clatters, lets the pump cycle too often and wears it faster. The float switch tells the pump when to start and stop, so a stuck or tangled float causes both no-start and nonstop running.

The discharge line carries water out of the house. In Minnesota winters, ice can plug the end of a discharge pipe and force the pump to run until the motor overheats. Keep the outlet clear of snow, make sure the pipe slopes away, and ask about a freeze-resistant discharge fitting.

Battery backup and water-powered backup

A battery backup pump takes over when the power fails, but it has limits. A typical battery runs the pump for a limited number of hours depending on how hard the water comes in, and the battery needs testing and replacement. A water-powered backup uses city water pressure to move water, needs no battery, but uses a good deal of water and depends on adequate pressure, and is not suited for every setup.

Neither replaces a healthy main pump. Test any backup once a year by pouring water into the pit.

What to check before you call

Look at the pit for standing water level and any debris. Listen for the motor and see if the float rises freely. Check that the pump is plugged in and the breaker has not tripped. Note the brand and age of the pump if you can read it, and clear any ice or snow from the discharge outlet.
